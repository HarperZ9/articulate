The morning was thick with the scent of salt and damp wood, the kind of stillness that settled over the sea when the world had forgotten to move. The sky was a pale blue, unbroken by clouds, and the sun rose slow and steady, its golden light spilling across the water like spilled honey. The ocean lay calm, a mirror polished by the hands of time, reflecting the pale blue of the sky and the pale faces of the crew. Not a ripple disturbed the surface, not even the faintest whisper of wind. It was as if the sea had paused to listen, to breathe, to remember the days when it was not a battlefield of steel and bone, but a vast and quiet expanse of mystery.

The whaler sat on the quarterdeck, her back against the rail, the weight of the morning pressing down on her like the silence that clung to the air. She had been on the water for months, but this was the first time she had seen the sea so still, so unbroken. The usual hum of the ship—the creak of the timbers, the groan of the mast, the distant cry of a gull—had softened into a hush, as though the ship itself was holding its breath.

The crew moved with quiet efficiency, their motions precise and unhurried. The cook stirred a pot of coffee, the steam rising in delicate curls, and the deckhands polished the brass fittings with the care of men who knew the sea could change in an instant. Even the captain, usually a man of sharp words and sharper eyes, stood at the helm with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the horizon as if expecting something to appear there—something he had not yet seen.

The sailor felt the weight of the moment settle in her chest. There was a strange beauty to the stillness, a kind of peace that came not from absence, but from the presence of something vast and unknowable. She thought of the whales, of the deep where they lay hidden, and wondered if they, too, were watching, waiting, just as she was. The sea was a great and silent watcher, and today, it had chosen to be still.
